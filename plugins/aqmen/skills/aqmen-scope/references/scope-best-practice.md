# What makes a scope win (and what makes it fail)

Condensed from MBB practice and from Aqmen's own proposals. Read this once before
drafting; it is short on purpose.

## The proposal confirms, it does not persuade

A scope that arrives before the client has said "yes, in principle" is doing the
selling job on its own, and it usually loses. The best scopes read as the written
record of a conversation that already happened: the client recognises their own
words in the problem statement. So the intake conversation matters more than the
prose. If the consultant only has a vague brief, say so and propose a short call
before writing.

## Structure: Situation → Complication → Resolution

- **Situation**: the client's business, the decision on the table, in their terms.
- **Complication**: why now, what's at stake, why the existing evidence is not enough.
- **Resolution**: the objectives and the approach — the biggest section.

Then: deliverables, workplan and timeline, team (argued for fit, not CVs),
commercials, why us, appendices. Keep it short. Meridian ran to six pages; that
is the right order of magnitude for a 3–6 week engagement.

## Be specific or be ignored

- "Strategic support" is not a deliverable. "A live market model with driver tree,
  trends and three scenarios, handed over in the final session" is.
- Every workstream: hypotheses → analyses → sources → outputs. A client should be
  able to tick each output off when it lands.
- Say what is **out of scope**. It protects the fee and it shows judgement.
- Fees show their working: fixed fee by phase or workstream, what's included, what
  is passed through, when it is invoiced. Case-by-case numbers, but always the logic.

## Differentiate on insight, not adjectives

The "why Aqmen" section works when it says something about *this* problem that a
generalist would not: a hypothesis the client hadn't considered, a data source
they didn't know existed, a benchmark from another market. One such moment per
section is the target. Adjectives ("rigorous", "world-class") are noise.

Aqmen's structural differentiators, to be tied to the specific engagement each
time rather than pasted:
- AI-first team of strategy consultants and technologists; more hypotheses tested,
  deeper.
- Proprietary platform for market, competition and company analysis, run by the
  people who built it.
- Live, queryable deliverables (models and a natural-language query layer) instead
  of a static report; the client runs its own scenarios.
- Co-created in real time; weeks to hours.

## Timeline that a client can put in a diary

Name the days. Two touchpoints a week works well for short engagements: a short
interim check midweek and a findings session at the end of the week. Show the
final session as the moment the client takes over the deliverables (a hands-on
walkthrough), not just a presentation.

## Common failure modes to check before sending

- Hypotheses that are really tasks ("assess pricing") — rewrite as assertions.
- A workstream with outputs but no analyses, or analyses with no source.
- Long-lead items (interviews, surveys, client data) not started in week 1.
- Nothing said about what the client must provide, or by when.
- Empty bullets, unfilled `[brackets]`, inconsistent project names, a fee that does
  not match the team × weeks in the workplan.
- Firm-centric language ("we will leverage our methodology") instead of
  client-centric ("Ares will be able to…").
